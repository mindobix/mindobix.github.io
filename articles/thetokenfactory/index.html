Building a token factory

Neil

The future of background agents

Why the best latency is no latency, what an unbounded market looks like, and the point at which a scientific answer costs tens of dollars.

PatrickWhy are you so confident in that? To me it seems like I want everything as fast as possible.

Neil

When you're waiting on it, you absolutely deserve the fastest answer possible. My trick is I don't want you to be waiting on it. I want it to be proactive. I want it to be in the background. One way to say it is: the best latency is no latency at all. When you wake up in the morning, the work's already been done overnight. You didn't even have to ask for it. That's the dream — we're not quite there yet.

But more importantly, the more you're in the loop as you prompt agents and wait for a response, the more you're the bottleneck in helping the agent do more or less work. What we'd like is for the agent to operate on more human time scales. You don't manage your colleagues every five minutes. You ask them to do a high-level task and you come back and check in maybe every day, but more likely once a week. That to me is the future of human-agent collaboration.

PatrickSay more about the early indications that this is happening — and therefore that you should be building this company.

Neil

The first and most important thing is the idea of test-time compute scaling: that you can give an agent more time and it will give you a better answer. That was theorized about two years ago now, but it wasn't really something we could actually bet on until, I'd say, late last year with Opus 4.5.

Opus 4.5 was the first agent that was at all suitable for longer-horizon tasks, and it was pretty mediocre when it first came out. But you look at the more recent models, and what we've done on open source as well, and you see agents are capable of running for an hour at a time. I wouldn't say days, but definitely an hour is quite suitable today.

Just seeing that average turn or task length get longer and longer — it doesn't take many points to draw out the exponential and see that agents are worth running for longer periods.

PatrickWhat do you think will be the market share of long-running agents in three years?

Neil

I love this market because it's unbounded. There's no human in the loop, so you can consume as many tokens as you like in the background — versus human attention span. If you tell me to consume 10x as many tokens at Codex or at Claude Code, I'm actually not sure that I can anymore. I'm already in a loop and locked in coding for most of the day that I'm at the laptop. What is unbounded is how many tokens can be consumed in the background or proactively.

So long term, I think we're going to end this year at maybe 50/50 background and real-time workloads, but I see this going to 90/10 in favor of background.

PatrickWhat are your favorite examples of something that gets accomplished much better as a background task than as a human-in-the-loop task?

Most deep research. Most questions where you want a definitive answer over not 100 sources, not a thousand sources, but 10,000 sources or more. That is the kind of crazy exabyte-scale task that needs a very different scale of intelligence to achieve.

Deep research is a top category for us, and increasingly we see cybersecurity following this direction. There's only so much code you can generate, but there are exponentially more ways to break that same code than there are to generate it. And there are some great customers out there working very hard on agents that can break any piece of software and proactively patch it.

These are all things you would write specialized agents for. You wouldn't just have Fable look at the source code once; you'd have it set up environments where you can pentest these applications.

At some point people started to make this joke that security has become proof of work. When you want secure software, it's really a question of how many dollars did you spend on Anthropic's APIs trying to break into your software. That is the best indication of how secure it is, because that's the best tool in the world.

And increasingly we found that the frontier of intelligence here is quite jagged. It's not the case that Fable finds a superset of all bugs in software. You'd find some bugs with a very small model that you don't find with the large model. You'd find some bugs with Haiku that you wouldn't find with Fable, and vice versa. So it encouraged this very diverse approach to sampling.

Neil

For individual users, what I'm most excited about is proactive intelligent agents. If you trust the machine enough that it's reliable and also trustworthy — as in private — you might even imagine the machine understanding how you interact with it and proactively surfacing your next action whenever you open your phone. Can we build a good model of what you're going to do next? My estimation is yes, we totally can. And the key to that is incredibly cheap intelligence. You have to be willing to spend tokens without any promise of return. That's the unlock.

The long-lens view is that we have a form of intelligence that can tackle any verifiable problem. Any verifiable problem means most software. It means a lot of formal math proofs and similar. And it could also mean scientific discovery. These are all relatively verifiable problems. And all those things currently have a dollar cost attached to them — a hidden one. How many tokens could you possibly harness to make this work?

We have started to bring within view a dollar cost for these long-horizon tasks that is reasonable. It's not millions, it's thousands — and maybe hundreds or even tens of dollars in the near future to have a definitive answer to any scientific question.

Pretty much. The models are on the cusp of taking even a high-level question and chasing down every possible follow-up. You can have the model take that on its own, and the question is just what is your token budget. And we will solve the token budget problem.

Chapter 03

Nvidia and the GPU stack

Tensor cores earning their die area, the milk club, "speed of light" — and why throughput and latency can never both be won.

PatrickLet's talk about the stack of solutions you hope to build to get to this giant token factory. You think about it in levels — software, hardware, power. Talk through the master plan.

Neil

We always have to start with software. Where is the opportunity, on today's chips and in today's data centers, to improve efficiency? The first thing we did was try to build the entire LLM software stack around peak GPU efficiency — we're using Nvidia GPUs, and we wanted to squeeze more tokens out of the same chip than anyone else in the world.

That starts with the lowest level of programming: kernels. It's actually my background. I spent my whole professional life working on GPUs and kernels. Nvidia was my first job while I was in college, and I got to see how the tensor cores earned their right to be on the chip. This is back in 2016.

PatrickDescribe what that means for the lay person.

Neil

A tensor core is a specialized unit on the GPU that accelerates matrix multiplication. Simple as that.

PatrickAnd why is matrix multiplication so important?

Neil

That's a great question. I cannot say that there is a divine truth of the universe that explains why matrix multiplies seem to be the atomic unit of computation. But one way I've heard it described is that it's a really succinct way to mix two blocks of numbers together and have them interact in some interesting way. That's as much as I can say about it. It is really convenient that linear algebra turns out to be a very compact representation of arbitrary relationships in data.

Nvidia, great graphics company obviously, had market share dominance in GPUs and gaming graphics for quite some time. Then starting in the mid-2010s they began these skunkworks projects to make the graphics processor more suitable for the machine learning tasks they were tracking.

I remember reading the lab notebooks of some of my managers when I was at Nvidia. They would visit these small ML conferences like ICML or NeurIPS at the time and just take note of these papers — oh, this deep learning thing seems to be catching on, and what's really interesting is that these grad students are using gaming Nvidia GPUs to train their large models. We should double-click on this and figure out what's going on here.

By 2015, 2016, at least Jensen had the conviction to double down on: this usage of our chips is only going to grow, let's start allocating more and more precious silicon die area to this capability that seems to be emerging. Let's put the first version of tensor cores on the chip.

So we're talking about taking this gaming chip, designed for painting pixels on a screen, and adapting it to do matrix multiplies. It was early, and you'd be competing against the graphics teams when you asked for more silicon area. In any chip company there's always competition for that — it's something the designers guard so carefully. You never want to invest in the wrong technology, because that's opportunity cost you could have allocated to some other functionality.

So we fought tooth and nail and got just a tiny bit of die area — maybe 5 or 10%, something like that — for the first generation of these chips, to get some acceleration for basic convolutions, which were the fundamental operation for computer vision models at the time.

Then we had a software team trying to squeeze all the performance we could out of the chip. That team, which is where I worked, taught me the most about the ethos Nvidia has. They have this term called speed of light. They always chase the speed of light for any piece of hardware they make. It is so ingrained in every engineer's mind that if the machine can do it, we're going to push the machine to the frontier.

PatrickAnd the speed of light is the edge of what's possible.

Neil

The speed of light is the edge of what's possible. Exactly. If we think the chip can run at this frequency and produce this many multiplies per cycle, we're going to get there. We're going to break every bottleneck and get to that peak level of performance.

To this day I tell all my engineers: we're chasing 100% speed of light. I don't care about relative numbers versus the competition. I only care about absolute numbers.
PatrickBefore we leave your time at Nvidia — anything else beyond that cultural touch point that changed how you think, or that stood out about how the business ran?

Neil

I have a ton of stories about Nvidia. One of my favorites is that on the tenure side, a lot of people I worked with in 2015, 2016 are still there today. That company has incredible retention, and these are the best engineers — frankly on the silicon side at least — I've worked with in my whole career. They're extremely motivated and passionate. They've believed in parallel computing as a concept through its various incarnations and have loved seeing the chip evolve. This is their life's work.

They're also a very frugal company. Nvidia — and I guess all the Silicon Valley companies after 2008 — had some cutbacks on perks. So, no free lunch. Nvidia took it one step further: there was no free milk in the fridge. If you wanted to drink coffee at Nvidia and you wanted some milk, you actually had to chip in a dollar every month to the milk club, and the milk club would stock Costco milk in the fridge. I remember that distinctly. We don't do that at Sail, but it's a frugality that permeates the company.

PatrickLink that experience — making more efficient use of the underlying hardware through software — to today's environment.

Neil

The GPU is fundamentally a throughput machine. It's happiest when you give it a lot of work to do and let it chew through that work at peak utilization of its compute units. But that's not the way we've taken AI in the last couple of years. We've really pushed AI to be an interactive chatbot tool — that's the most common form of AI usage today. And in that world you care a lot about spitting answers out to the person at the keyboard as quickly as possible.

That's quite interesting for the GPU. It's very difficult to put the GPU in its happy path of being fully compute-utilized when you're trying to spit out tokens quickly. There's a fundamental trade-off on the GPU between being throughput oriented or latency optimized, and everyone has chosen latency optimization because the shape of usage was chatbot oriented. I believe that's the most profound change we're going to see in the next year — away from chatbots, towards proactive or background agents. In that world it makes a lot more sense to build a stack around throughput.

PatrickExplain technically why the trade-off between throughput and latency is unbreakable. Why can't we have both from the same hardware?

Neil

It's quite foundational in almost every system you could ever look at. There's always a trade-off between getting a small amount of data through the system as quickly as possible, and running wide and slow. Narrow and fast or wide and slow is a classic trade-off in all computer science.

For GPUs specifically, focus on this concept of batching. We want to group many users' work together into a batch that we can run all at once on the GPU. That's the parallel processing of the GPU — we'd like to have a lot of parallel work to do. The thing is, you're doing net more work when you run a large batch of compute together. You might be filling all the units, but every step along the way as you carry a batch through the GPU, there's more work to be done. So any individual token, or any individual user's request in that batch, is going to spend a longer time on the GPU being carried along with other people's traffic.

Maybe the way to say it is: if you want to get downtown in SF, you can take the bus or you can take private transit. The private transit has its own direct path, as the crow flies, using exactly the roads you want from point A to point B. A bus has to serve many more people, and it has to do something that works for everyone — so it takes a slower path, and it stops and waits for other people to get on and off.

PatrickThe bus versus car analogy is a good one. So step one for you is to create the best possible bus on top of Nvidia GPUs.

Neil

That's exactly right. It means we explore things like different parallelism schemes.

Another example: one of the things Nvidia has really innovated on is the NVLink interconnect between GPUs. That system is so good that if you have a large matrix multiply you want to perform faster, you can cut it in half and shard it across two or more — up to eight, let's say — Nvidia GPUs, have them all work on pieces of that larger matrix multiply, and reduce their results back together at the end.

This is a great way to cut the minimum latency of an operation. Each GPU is now doing one-eighth as much work, so it can finish faster — but not eight times faster. It's sublinear scaling. You'll use eight times more hardware, but you might only get four to five times the speed. You're not going to get strong scaling. That's because of communication overhead, and because every GPU is a little less efficient working on a smaller tile of work than a larger one.

It's the only way to speed up if you want the minimum latency possible. You can do that, but it is not the choice I would make. I'd prefer a different parallelism scheme like expert parallelism or pipeline parallelism, and we may do interesting things to overlap and hide the communication latency in a way you'd have less ability to do for a low-latency server.

PatrickSo is the right way to think about NVLink as a technology that improves latency performance?

Neil

Yes — and only latency performance, which segues into what we do differently as a company. NVLink is mandatory, I'd say, for low-latency inference. So Nvidia is excellent at low-latency inference. And I'm telling you that we don't really care that much about low-latency inference.

So where does that leave us? I'm not holding my breath for other companies broadly to figure out NVLink quickly. It's a challenging technology to figure out. It's hard to scale, hard to productionize. So if I have some other vendor's chip and it's good at the foundational compute components — it can still do matrix multiplies really well, it just can't communicate those results across its peers quickly — well, maybe there's room for that chip in my stack as a really good compute-per-dollar option.

That's what I actually optimize for in most cases: how many flops does this chip have, and how much is it going to cost me per hour to own and operate. There are other chips that definitely rank higher than Nvidia on flops per dollar, but they may not have as much interconnect. So it's my job to figure out what parallelism scheme makes this chip suitable for inference. It's not going to be tensor parallelism — Nvidia is basically mandatory for that. But other techniques may work well for me.

Chapter 04

Chips, memory, and transformers

SRAM against DRAM, what Cerebras is really buying with a whole wafer, and the original sin of the transformer.

PatrickBefore we leave latency — what do you make of Cerebras and others that can perform incredibly fast operations? What is your prediction for the future of very low-latency-focused hardware?

Neil

Cerebras, Groq, and a couple of others coming out of stealth now have made a very interesting bet: not just building another GPU, but building a different kind of accelerator that focuses on a different memory hierarchy. They want to maximize the amount of SRAM on the chip and use that as very fast memory for weights and KV cache.

There are two ways to make memory for a chip. One is to integrate the memory on the logic die itself — you tell TSMC, I want this many megabytes of storage on my chip. TSMC has a standard cell library, and you can print out a bunch of cells of SRAM. The problem with SRAM is it takes a lot of area on the silicon die. If you want to build a large die — say the Nvidia Blackwell at 800 mm² — and you made that whole thing SRAM, it would be in maybe the single-digit gigabytes. Not a crazy amount of storage.

Compare that to taking a different process technology entirely. Not TSMC anymore, but Micron, SK Hynix, Samsung. They build DRAM, which is a whole different way to build memory, more focused on capacitors than transistor cells.

SRAM — the standard way to build it is what's called the 6T transistor cell. It's a stable transistor arrangement that lets you write a bit to it and then it holds that state. You do have to apply some power, but it's holding that bit without any active management. It's static.

Dynamic RAM is dynamic because to write data you write a charge onto a capacitor, and as soon as you write that charge, it starts dissipating. It's leaking. So the dynamic part of DRAM is that you must, every 50 milliseconds or so, refresh every bit you've written. You're constantly juggling billions of balls in the air — billions of bits managed by a memory controller reading and refreshing every bit on the DRAM.

The benefit is you get much higher density. It's a whole different process technology with a ton of different trade-offs — hence why DRAM manufacturing sits in entirely different companies like Micron, SK Hynix, and Samsung. These are the best companies in the world to do this.

If you take DRAM from those companies, stack it into many layers, and solder it around the main logic die from Nvidia, you can get hundreds of gigabytes. Blackwell has 288 GB of HBM capacity around the logic die, and the logic die itself maybe only has 500 megabytes of SRAM. So it's possibly three orders of magnitude difference in density, DRAM versus SRAM.

PatrickSo let's go back to Cerebras. What are they doing?

Neil

They see this problem — there's no obvious way to increase SRAM density on the chip. But the thing with SRAM is that because it's so physically close to the logic gates that do the computation — the arithmetic logic units are right next to the SRAM they pull from — the compute units doing the matrix multiplies can pull data from SRAM at just mind-boggling speeds. Cerebras quotes 21 petabytes per second for Wafer Scale Engine 3. Compare that to HBM on an Nvidia Blackwell, which is around 10 terabytes per second. Once again, orders of magnitude: more capacity, but proportionally less bandwidth.

So what Cerebras does is say: we're going to take as many of these dies as we can. We're not going to limit ourselves to the 800 square millimeter reticle limit TSMC imposes. We're going to take the entire wafer and have every die connect to every other die over scribe lines, and get as much SRAM as we can on the whole wafer — let's say 50 gigabytes per wafer. Then we stack many wafers together in a pipeline, and now we can have up to a terabyte of very fast memory.

And you do all that work just to get the ability to read data from SRAM at 21 petabytes per second per wafer. Therefore you can serve these language models at extremely high tokens per second, because you can move the entire parameter count of a large model like Kimi in and off the logic cores in about a millisecond. So there you go — you have a path to a thousand tokens per second.

PatrickAnd what's your prediction for that segment of the market?

Neil

I think what happens is some hybrid outcome, where we pair the Cerebras chip where it's very strong — very fast access to memory — with something that has more capacity for memory. It's true you can take a one-trillion-parameter model like Kimi and fit it on a large number of Cerebras wafers. But you can't do something about the KV cache very easily. The KV cache grows as people use the model more, and it's always dynamic. You don't even know how much you're going to need. It depends on how many users you have and how many you want to serve.

PatrickCan you explain KV cache in basic terms?

Neil

Whenever you use a language model, every token you send through stays in the context window for as long as you're having a conversation. If we talk for 100,000 tokens, the 100,001st token is still in the conversation behind us, and the model references all the past conversation history to make better predictions about what the next thing we're going to say is.

So that KV cache is a bunch of memory. You have to store a representation for every token you send through the language model, and it frequently gets larger than the weights of the model themselves. You have this crystallized knowledge in the model weights, and the dynamic knowledge of the exact conversation we're having in the KV cache. That's how I like to think about it.

PatrickAnd this is why people observe that deep in a conversation things start to degrade — some sort of technical problem.

Neil

The KV cache is quite interesting in that regard. It's an exact representation of everything that came before — we store all the information we've seen in the conversation. However, during training, the model did not get trained primarily on very long context conversations. It got trained primarily on, let's say, 8,000-token or 16,000-token conversations. So if you take the model to 200,000 tokens, there was some training at that context length, but it's not the model's core strength.

There's always been a challenge for the frontier labs: how do we make the model exactly as intelligent at 10,000 tokens as we expect it to be at 200,000 tokens? It's going to be a perennial battle. We've had one-million context windows as a concept for years now — Anthropic was, I think, the first to hit that length. I still use /compact in my Claude Code well before one million context length. I don't think it's actually great to hit the full length.

PatrickSo these extremely fast, extremely low-latency approaches are ultimately limited by this factor.

Neil

Yes. You can do whatever you want for the weights — it's very possible to have unbeatable performance on weight storage. However, the KV cache is going to be a big thorn in your side.

PatrickThree years from now, five years from now, what role do these kinds of chips play? What market share do they have in the heterogeneous chip market?

Neil

Cerebras and Groq and maybe a couple of others — you should think of them as accelerators. What they're really good at is being used in conjunction with a more traditional GPU-like device that critically has off-chip memory built in. You want off-chip memory for capacity and on-chip memory for speed. We want to hybridize these two things.

If you take transformers in the limit — take a transformer to a million context length — what happens is you have this compute-bound stage, which is the actual matrix multiplies for the MLP, where most of the model's world knowledge is encoded. And then you have the attention layer, where we're dynamically adapting to the current conversation. Attention in the limit is usually memory bound, and the MLP in the limit is compute bound at large enough batch size.

The original sin of transformers is that you've taken this fundamentally memory-bound layer and juxtaposed it right next to a compute-bound layer.

It is very difficult to have a single chip that is good at both compute operations and memory operations. The GPU is quite balanced in this regard, but you have to choose one or the other. Cerebras has very fast memory access, and it's really good to host the MLP — the weights essentially — on the Cerebras chip. But the GPU has the capacity to scale to really long context lengths, so you'd like to put attention on the GPU and the MLP on the Cerebras chip. I believe this is what's happening with Nvidia and Groq.

PatrickRiff for a minute on transformers. What was the innovation in 2017, what are its strengths and weaknesses, and will it remain the dominant architecture?

Neil

What it did was allow us to learn on unsupervised data really effectively. Transformers, at the end of the day, are about taking any arbitrary sequence of data and trying to find patterns in it. And critically, the attention operation — the headline component of transformers — allows the model to dynamically adapt to what it thinks is the most relevant component of the sequence. Every step you take through a transformer, you're essentially reweighing the input you looked at before and figuring out which is most relevant for your next prediction.

So it's extremely amenable to learning arbitrary sequence data. And the most interesting sequences of data that we produce on a regular basis is language — that's how we got to dominance in the language regime.

To zoom out further, what transformers really did well is that they scaled. Transformers make no such human prior. They just say: there's going to be a pattern in the sequence of data, and if there is one, I'm going to find it. I'm going to throw more and more parameters at this problem until it works.

Transformers benefited from a lot of the computer vision work too. One of the challenges in computer vision was that we had a hard time going from the hundreds of thousands of parameters you get for linear models like support vector machines. Then we got to deep learning and tens of millions of parameters — the biggest models were around 150 million parameters, which was huge for computer vision. And now we routinely talk about trillions of parameters. Transformers are the link to go from millions to trillions.

PatrickIf the important units of scaling are data and compute, does it stand to reason that transformers stick around, because that's the thing we're good at getting more of?

Neil

Data is an open question, but compute — yeah. Transformers are just such great sponges. You increase the compute available to a transformer by 10x and you'll get some log improvement somewhere. So far the scaling laws really work. They're really quite beautiful.

And what do transformers do really well? They extend to almost any data set you can throw at them. They're extremely powerful general learners. What's especially useful about transformers over other techniques we've tried to replace attention with is that transformers represent any pairwise relationship you want. This is another trick we learned in the computer vision days. Once you overfit the data set, you can work backwards and find the general patterns that fit into the smallest parameter count possible.

Analysis · What he believes

Thirteen convictions, and what he leans on to hold them

Neil's argument is unusually load-bearing: nearly every claim below depends on the one above it. If background agents don't take over, the throughput bet is wrong; if the throughput bet is wrong, the cheap-chip arbitrage has no home; if that fails, the scavenged megawatt is stranded. Tags mark where he is knowingly offside from the market.

01Contrarian

Inference is about to stop being a chat window

The dominant shape of AI usage moves from interactive chatbot to proactive, long-running background agent — and that changes what hardware you should want.

What he leans onTest-time compute scaling was theorized two years ago but only became bettable in late 2025 with Opus 4.5, the first model he considered suitable for long-horizon work. Agents now run about an hour; he says it takes few points to draw the exponential. He puts the split at roughly 50/50 at year-end, heading to 90/10.
The best latency is no latency at all. When you wake up in the morning, the work's already been done overnight.
02Consensus mechanism, contrarian application

Throughput and latency can never both be won

Narrow-and-fast versus wide-and-slow is foundational, not an engineering gap waiting to close. Everyone optimized for latency because usage was chat-shaped. He builds the bus.

What he leans onBatching does net more total work; each request rides along with other people's traffic. NVLink can shard a matrix multiply across eight GPUs, but scaling is sublinear — 8x the hardware buys maybe 4–5x the speed, lost to communication overhead and smaller, less efficient tiles.
NVLink is mandatory for low-latency inference. And I'm telling you that we don't really care that much about low-latency inference.
03Contrarian

There are no bad chips, only bad pricing

If you don't need NVLink, the whole ranking of silicon changes. He optimizes for flops per dollar per hour and finds a parallelism scheme to fit whatever he buys.

What he leans onHis kernel team's ability to extract performance where vendors do less work than Nvidia does out of the box — plus the market's perception gap on AMD, which he calls "music to my ears." He names TPUs, Trainium, Etched, SambaNova and d-Matrix as things he'll make work, and concedes AMD supply is now being allocated too.
I will buy any chip anywhere in the world for any duration of time.
04Contrarian

Aggregate power beats concentrated power

Gigawatt sites are effectively unbuildable in the US and 100 MW is close behind — but 1 MW is plentiful and nobody serious wants it. Inference, unlike training, doesn't need one campus.

What he leans onLiquid cooling collapsed a megawatt from a warehouse-sized data hall into eight refrigerator-sized racks. Training demanded co-location because nobody wants cross-data-center training; inference doesn't. He strips out diesel backup, redundant networking and multiple fiber lines as pure cost.
We're trying to build the best steel factory in the world. But it will come through mini mills, not through large monolithic steel plants.
05Contrarian

P99 latency is the right thing to sell off

He will buy 95% uptime — possibly 80% at the right price — and pass the savings on. The trade he offers customers is explicit: competitive average throughput, uncontrolled tail latency, unbeatable economics.

What he leans onA control plane that re-homes a failed request to another GPU, so long as failures aren't correlated across sites. He describes himself as "linearly happy" from 95% to 99% uptime. A stranded agent turn costs minutes, against a job measured in hours.
My customers don't care, because their agent was running for hours. They're sleeping.
06His framing

The transformer has an original sin

Attention is memory-bound in the limit; the MLP is compute-bound at large batch. The architecture bolts them together, and no single chip is good at both.

What he leans onThe memory hierarchy gap: Cerebras quotes 21 PB/s of SRAM bandwidth on Wafer Scale Engine 3, against roughly 10 TB/s of HBM on Blackwell — but Blackwell carries 288 GB against a logic die's ~500 MB of SRAM. His resolution is hybridization: weights on the wafer-scale part, attention and its unbounded KV cache on the GPU.
The original sin of transformers is that you've taken this fundamentally memory-bound layer and juxtaposed it right next to a compute-bound layer.
07Contrarian

The KV cache is the fattest target in the stack

Compute scaling is already efficient and mixture-of-experts models are already sparse — under 10% dense, frontier models nearer 1%. The waste is in attention's use of memory.

What he leans onKilobytes stored per token, which he estimates is off by one to two orders of magnitude on an entropy basis. DeepSeek's published compression work is making order-of-magnitude progress roughly yearly, which he reads as evidence the headroom is real rather than theoretical.
If you look at the entropy in a KV cache, it's nowhere near earning its keep.
08Contrarian

The world's compute is badly marshalled

The shortage is partly an orchestration failure. Nvidia ships five million Blackwells this year and a great many of them are not working most of the time.

What he leans onCompute that disappears into private pools and never sees the light of day. He notes that xAI takes public criticism for cluster utilization while "the reality for the rest of the world is far, far worse."
It actually pains me physically to see that those GPUs are just sitting idle. I want to fix that.
09Consensus — he says so himself

The internet was a one-time subsidy; environments are the next block

Roughly 30 trillion tokens of high-quality text, 300 trillion on a looser definition, and models have been through all of it repeatedly. The next phase is self-improvement inside verifiable RL gyms.

What he leans onThe spend shift at frontier labs from data acquisition to RL environments. And an argument that generic human preference data has been outgrown — the median served model now beats the median rater, so only expert preference carries signal. He calls this "not a super differentiated take."
If you want artificial general intelligence, the best way to get there is to just keep stacking specialized intelligences until you have no more gaps to fill.
10Contrarian

Distillation cannot be prevented, only delayed

He reframes the theft debate: even with zero intent to scrape, capability diffuses, because a growing share of what humans publish was written by models.

What he leans onGitHub — what fraction of repos created in the last year came out of Claude Code? He suspects a Fable-class model could be trained on nothing but open-source code judged good, and notes users own their outputs and publish them freely. He calls the result "latent distillation."
I don't think it's fundamentally possible to prevent the diffusion of information or model capabilities. It will happen. The question is just how fast.
11Contrarian

Losing TSMC would hurt far less than people think

The geopolitical panic assumes each process node is a large performance step. On the metric he cares about, it isn't.

What he leans onPerformance per watt on a BF16 multiply has barely moved across Hopper, Blackwell and Rubin, and doesn't change dramatically from 5 nm to 4 to 3 to 2. He puts Western processes like Intel at worst around 2x behind — a supply shock, not a capability collapse.
My contrarian take is that it wouldn't be that bad.
12Contested

This is not the dot-com build-out

Semis have gone from 2–4% of the S&P 500 to around 20%, and the market expects mean reversion. He thinks the analogy breaks on one point: what the spend is for.

What he leans onNetworking capex in 2000 anticipated users who never arrived. The 2023–24 Hopper crunch was training spend, which is inherently speculative. Inference spend is not — companies are now capping how much engineers may spend on Claude Code, which is the opposite of a demand problem.
You don't hoard tokens, you use them immediately.
13Article of faith

Demand for intelligence is unbounded

The ceiling isn't appetite, it's on-ramps — and he's candid that solving on-ramps is somebody else's job.

What he leans onLess evidence than conviction, and he says as much. His supporting observation is a commercial one: customers repeatedly tell him they can't afford to give free-tier users enough tokens. He also concedes "I'm not a product person, so I cannot say I have the best vision."
I never will believe in that. There is always demand for intelligence in the world.

Analysis · What is still unsolved

Twenty hard problems he names, and who is on the hook for each

Neil is unusually specific about where the walls are. Three he returns to unprompted, more than once: the entropy sitting uncompressed in the KV cache, what would have to change architecturally to offload that cache to flash, and the fact that a large share of the world's GPUs are doing nothing right now. Those are the ones he says he whiteboards. The rest he raises in passing — several while explaining why something else works.

Layer 01

Architecture and intelligence

The problems he cannot fix himself, because he sits downstream of whoever designs the model.

01OpenFrontier labs

The KV cache stores kilobytes per token and earns almost none of it

He points at the entropy: measured against the information actually in it, the cache is off by an order of magnitude or two. It routinely grows larger than the model weights themselves, and it scales with users rather than with the model — so you cannot provision for it in advance.

Why it's hardUnlike weights, the cache is dynamic and unbounded. It's the reason a wafer-scale part can hold a trillion-parameter model and still not be able to serve it — you can size the weights, you can't size the conversation. DeepSeek's published compression work is the evidence he cites that the headroom is real; the fact that it moves an order of magnitude a year is also the evidence that nobody has bottomed it out.
If you look at the entropy in a KV cache, it's nowhere near earning its keep.
02OpenSail — his own obsession

What would have to change about the model to put the KV cache on flash?

This is the idea he says makes well-informed friends look at him strangely, and the one he whiteboards constantly. Get the cache off HBM and onto a much cheaper, much slower tier of memory, and the entire HBM shortage stops being his problem.

Why it's hardIt isn't a systems change, it's an architecture change — flash is orders of magnitude slower than HBM, so the model itself would have to tolerate that access pattern. And he doesn't own the architecture. He needs a frontier lab to want the same thing, or the change has to come from the open-source side he serves.
Everyone on my team knows I keep banging the drum around: what would we have to change about the model architecture to make offloading KV cache to flash work at a much greater level?
03OpenFrontier labs

Models are not as intelligent at 200,000 tokens as at 10,000

The cache is an exact record of everything that came before, but the model was trained mostly on conversations of 8,000 or 16,000 tokens. Long context is real capacity without proportionate competence.

Why it's hardHe calls it a perennial battle rather than a bug — the training distribution and the serving distribution simply don't match, and stretching one to cover the other has resisted years of work. His own behaviour is the tell: million-token windows have existed for years and he still runs /compact in Claude Code well before he gets near the limit.
I don't think it's actually great to hit the full length.
04May be unsolvableNobody

Everything non-verifiable — starting with human taste

His entire theory of progress runs through verifiability: give a model a hard task and a self-grading system, and you have a recipe for self-improvement on anything. The corollary is a category that recipe simply cannot reach.

Why it's hardThere is no grader. Software, formal proofs and much of scientific discovery are verifiable, so they're on the ramp. The quality of writing and the beauty of art have no self-grading system to hand the model, and he doesn't claim to see one coming — this is the single problem in the conversation he suggests may not be soluble in principle.
We have not solved human taste yet, and I don't know that it fundamentally can be. I'm excited to be surprised here.
05Structural constraintFrontier labs

He has to guess the architecture before he can buy the hardware

Sparse attention or dense. BF16, FP8 or FP4. Choices that look arbitrary from inside a lab determine which chips are viable for him and how he should build his serving stack — and he has no input into any of them.

Why it's hardHardware commitments run years; architecture choices land in months. He is fully downstream, buying long-lived physical capacity against decisions other people haven't made yet. His stated method is prediction, and his stated fallback is hope.
I can only pray that they go in the direction that is amenable to me — or I have to do my best to predict where they're going and build my serving architecture accordingly.
Layer 02

Silicon and memory

Where physics and the supply chain set limits that no amount of software will move.

06OpenChip vendors

No single chip is good at both compute and memory

The transformer's two halves want opposite machines. The MLP is compute-bound at large batch; attention in the limit is memory-bound. They sit adjacent in every layer, and you have to pick.

Why it's hardThe memory hierarchy forces the trade at the process level: SRAM is fast because it's on the logic die, and it's small for exactly the same reason — Blackwell's logic die carries maybe 500 MB against 288 GB of stacked HBM around it. Cerebras answers with 21 PB/s across a whole wafer and then can't hold a growing KV cache. His proposed fix is disaggregation across two kinds of silicon, which means somebody has to make them cooperate.
It is very difficult to have a single chip that is good at both compute operations and memory operations.
07Physical limitEveryone

Splitting work across GPUs never scales the way you want

Shard a matrix multiply across eight GPUs and you get four to five times the speed, not eight. The gap is communication overhead plus the fact that every GPU is less efficient on a small tile than a large one.

Why it's hardIt's the same wall as throughput-versus-latency, which he calls foundational to almost every system — narrow and fast or wide and slow, pick one. There is no version of this where more hardware buys proportionate speed, so the only real decision is which side of the trade your business sits on.
You'll use eight times more hardware, but you won't get eight times the speed. You're not going to get strong scaling.
08He has a routeSail

Making chips work when they can't talk to each other

NVLink is what makes low-latency inference possible, and he doesn't expect anyone else to replicate it soon. So every cheap chip he buys arrives with good compute and poor interconnect, and he has to find a parallelism scheme that tolerates that.

Why it's hardTensor parallelism is off the table without NVLink, so he's pushed to expert and pipeline parallelism and to hiding communication latency behind other work — techniques that only make sense because he's given up on the tail. Each new chip is a fresh instance of the problem, and the answer is different every time.
It's my job to figure out what parallelism scheme is going to make this chip suitable for inference.
09OpenFabs

Memory capacity cannot be conjured, and the memory makers have been burned

Asked what he'd attack if he were building silicon, he names HBM — and then explains why nobody will fix it quickly. There's no easy way to bring on a lot more memory fabs.

Why it's hardIt's a capital-cycle problem wearing a technical costume. DRAM makers have been punished repeatedly for large capex into a cyclical market, so they under-build on purpose. His expectation is that the shortage doesn't get routed around but passed on — phones lose memory, phones get more expensive, everyone absorbs it.
The boys in Boise don't love huge capex for cyclical businesses. They've been burned on that many times.
10Commercial, not technicalFabs

Fabs sell uniformity he may not need

Every production line has spread between its best and worst die. TSMC spends enormous effort tightening those process corners — controls that cost time and money to deliver a guarantee he'd happily trade away.

Why it's hardIt requires a supplier to expose its trade-offs to a customer, which is not how the most conservative layer of the stack operates. He'd take more rejects and worse corners for a better price, but there is no mechanism for him to ask.
If a fab exposes more of their trade-offs to me, I'm able to make more intelligent decisions.
Layer 03

Systems and orchestration

The layer he actually competes on — and the one where he is most willing to say the work isn't done.

11Open raceEveryone

Nobody yet knows how to program a whole rack as one computer

The unit of compute moved. You no longer program a GPU, or a motherboard — Nvidia now ships NVL72, a rack of 72 units, and the right mental model is the rack, maybe the cluster, maybe the entire data center.

Why it's hardHe calls it an open race explicitly, which is notable given how confident he is elsewhere. Whoever programs rack-scale most efficiently wins both ends of the trade — Nvidia's part is currently the best answer for lowest latency and highest throughput, so there's no cheap way to sidestep the problem by picking a lane.
It's an open race to figure out who can program the whole rack-scale computer as efficiently as possible.
12He has a routeSail

The GPU is only near its limit in a place you rarely are

In its happy path — a large-dimension matrix multiply — a modern GPU runs at 70 to 80% of peak, and what stops it there isn't software but power and thermals. The problem is that a transformer doesn't spend most of its time in the happy path.

Why it's hardThe published peak flops number is already optimistic, since you throttle before you reach it. So the real work isn't making the fast case faster — it's building an engine around the chip that keeps large batches arriving continuously, which is a scheduling and batching problem rather than a kernel one.
Our job is to build the engine around the chip such that we are feeding the GPU these large batches of work at all times.
13OpenThe market

The world's compute is not marshalled, and much of it is idle

Nvidia ships five million Blackwells this year. He doubts they are all being used all the time, and thinks the shortage is partly an orchestration failure rather than a manufacturing one.

Why it's hardThe compute disappears into private pools that will never see the light of day — allocated to a customer, sitting in a warehouse, contractually invisible. It's a market-design problem more than a technical one, and he notes that xAI takes public criticism for utilization while the rest of the world is quietly far worse.
At some level we just need better orchestration of compute across the world. This is very difficult to do.
14OpenSail

Correlated failure is the case his design doesn't cover

His whole economic argument rests on buying 95% uptime cheaply and re-homing failed requests elsewhere. The control plane handles any single failure in any single data center — with one condition attached.

Why it's hardThe condition is that failures aren't correlated across sites, and the strategy deliberately concentrates exposure to things that correlate: weather over a solar fleet, a regional grid, a single class of scavenged hardware. He states the caveat plainly and doesn't say what happens when it's violated.
It's going to be fine handling any single failure in any single data center, as long as it's not correlated with other data centers.
15OpenSail + frontier labs

Kernels break the moment a model leaves the happy path

Modern transformers are built on flash attention. But change how positional information is embedded — a tweak to the rope system — and the kernel you had no longer fits the model you're serving.

Why it's hardTwo things compound. The work is unavoidable, because model architectures keep moving and he serves whatever ships. And the obvious escape — have models write the kernels — isn't fully there yet, which he flags without being able to explain why. He now designs kernels on a whiteboard and hands the strategy to a model to implement, and expects the labs are already further along.
I'm not sure exactly why models are not superb at doing this.
Layer 04

Power and the physical world

The constraints that no longer bend to engineering at all — only to where you're willing to put the building.

16Constraint he routes aroundThe United States

You can no longer build concentrated power in America

A gigawatt data center is effectively impossible to build in the US now. A hundred megawatts is increasingly hard — basically impossible unless you are a very special set of customers. Ten megawatts is the edge of what's possible.

Why it's hardThere's a diseconomy of scale working against you: 100,000 GPUs in one place costs far more per unit than 10,000, which costs more than 1,000. And the industry's habits were formed by training, which assumed co-location because nobody wants cross-data-center training. The power exists — it just isn't in one place, and the market still hasn't repriced that.
There's this incredible lore on the market where you can find lots of aggregate power, but it will not be concentrated.
17He has a routeSail

Intermittent power against a persistent base load

Solar and wind are, in his view, badly undertapped in the US — and conventionally considered unsuitable for data centers for one reason. The load never stops; the supply does.

Why it's hardThe nightmare case for a data center is a long outage — fog over the valley for days, no wind for a week. His answer is that this is tolerable precisely because it's predictable: model the weather, know when the site goes dark, move the workload. That converts a physics problem into a forecasting-and-scheduling problem, and it only works because his customers are asleep.
I am totally capable of tolerating an outage for my data center that's measured in days or even weeks.
18His test for anyone elseChip startups

Four bottlenecks, and you need an answer to all of them

Asked what advice he'd give a room of would-be compute entrepreneurs, he doesn't talk about architecture. He asks whether they understand the three to five constraints that dictate modern chip supply: TSMC wafer capacity, HBM capacity, advanced packaging, and power.

Why it's hardRelieving one bottleneck just exposes the next — make 20% more chips and something else binds immediately. So a new chip only makes sense as arbitrage against a choice Nvidia finds difficult to reverse. His framing: Nvidia isn't perfect, it's well balanced, so the only way through is to be deliberately spiky somewhere.
You want to pick something and say: I think they've underpriced how short we're going to be on HBM.
Layer 05

Beyond engineering

Three he raises and then explicitly hands to somebody else.

19ConstraintNvidia

Getting compute at all is a relationship problem, not a price problem

Nvidia could clear the shortage by raising prices until supply and demand intersect. It deliberately doesn't, because letting the deepest pockets buy everything creates a customer who accrues too much power.

Why it's hardIt means allocation is discretionary, and a young company asking to rent ten thousand Blackwells for five years has months of operating history to point at. He says you need either exceptional relationships or extraordinary financial backing — neither of which is something you can engineer your way to.
They understand that compute is power today, and so they're quite strategic about how they allocate compute.
20OpenFrontier labs

The free data is gone and the cheap feedback is worthless

The internet was a one-time subsidy — about 30 trillion tokens of high-quality text, 300 trillion on a looser definition, and models have been through all of it many times. Worse, the fallback has stopped working.

Why it's hardInteraction data used to be the answer: people use a chatbot, they signal what they liked. But the median served model has now outgrown the median rater, so unconditioned human preference carries no signal — you need expert preference, which doesn't scale the same way. His replacement is RL environments, which is why lab spend has shifted there, and which only works where the task is verifiable.
There is not a whole lot more to be done on human data from the internet.
21Not his to solveProduct people

On-ramps — and trusting a machine that reads everything

He wants a proactive agent that has read all your email and messages and can surface your next action before you ask. He also concedes the hardest parts of that are outside his competence.

Why it's hardTwo gaps sit between cheap tokens and that product. Reliability and privacy have to hold simultaneously — the machine must be trustworthy in the sense of accurate and in the sense of private. And someone has to design the on-ramps that let ordinary people spend intelligence they've never had access to. He names both and steps back from both.
I'm not a product person, so I cannot say I have the best vision.
