The memory ladder
and why it breaks

Four kinds of memory do the work inside every AI answer. They are not variations on a theme — they differ by factors of a thousand. Once you can feel those gaps, the whole chip industry starts making sense.

Start here: four rungs, fastest at the top

Every rung down is bigger, cheaper, and dramatically slower. Engineers don't choose one — they use all four at once, and the entire art is deciding what lives where.

The bit that surprises everyone

HBM — the scarce, expensive memory the whole industry is short of — takes longer to answer than the ordinary RAM in your laptop. It is not bought for speed of response. It is bought for how much it can move at once.

Latency and bandwidth are not the same thing

This is the single most useful distinction on the page, and the one most explanations skip. Think of water: latency is how long before anything comes out of the tap. Bandwidth is how much pours out once it does.

Why HBM exists at all

Notice HBM sits mid-pack on response time but near the top on flow rate. That is the entire product: it answers no faster than normal RAM, but once it starts, it delivers roughly twenty times more per second. AI work reads gigantic amounts of data continuously, so flow rate is what matters and response time barely registers.

What the model is actually reaching for

To produce a single word, the machine must read two completely different things — and it must read both for every word it writes. They behave nothing alike.

Thing one

The weights

Everything the model learned in training — its knowledge of language and the world, frozen into numbers. Enormous, but a fixed size forever. You can measure it once and plan around it.

Thing two

The KV cache

The model's memory of this particular conversation — a record of every word so far, so it can refer back. It grows with every word and every user, and you cannot know in advance how big it will get.

Both have to sit in the fast memory, because both are read constantly. The weights move in once and stay put. The cache keeps swelling underneath them — and that is where the ladder starts to break.

The uncomfortable part

The conversation memory frequently ends up larger than everything the model ever learned. Worse, you can't size it in advance — it depends on how many people show up and how long they talk. So the most expensive, scarcest rung on the ladder is being consumed by the one thing nobody can predict.

That is the whole thing. And notice what's missing: nothing here shrinks as the conversation gets longer. Every additional word costs exactly as much as the last, forever. Movva calls this linear memory complexity — the least dramatic phrase for the most consequential property on this page.

Put a number on it

At 256 KB per word, a single 200,000-word conversation occupies about 52 GB — more than a fifth of an entire $30,000 accelerator's fast memory, devoted to remembering one user's chat.

Three levers, and each one halves it

Every term in that formula is a design choice someone made. Change them and the cost per word drops fast — which is precisely where the research energy is going.

The asymmetry Movva points at

The industry has worked hard to shrink model weights — some now ship at 4 bits per number. Yet the conversation cache is almost always left at 16 bits, untouched. One half of memory has been optimised for years; the other half has barely been looked at. That gap is the opportunity.

Why long conversations are so expensive

Here is the consequence that reaches your bill. A server has one pool of fast memory. Whatever the conversations don't take, the business can use to serve more people at once. Long chats eat that pool alive.

The same hardware serves ninety-six short conversations or three long ones. Nothing about the machine changed — only how much each user is asking it to remember. Movva notes that past roughly 200k it "gets hairy": batching collapses, and the cache starts to dominate the traffic moving through the chip.

So that's why long context costs more

When a provider charges extra beyond a context limit, this chart is the reason. You are no longer renting a slice of a machine — past a certain length you are effectively renting the whole machine.

So why not move it down a rung?

This is the idea Neil Movva says he whiteboards constantly. Flash costs about 250× less per gigabyte than HBM and nobody is fighting over it. Move the conversation memory down there and the shortage stops being your problem. One thing stands in the way.

The gap you'd have to survive

Flow rate · linear, not log
HBM
8 TB/s
Flash

That second bar is not a rendering error. Drawn to true scale, flash moves about 570 times less per second. The cache is read for every word, at every layer — so you cannot simply relocate it and hope. The model itself has to stop needing it so urgently.

What would have to change

  1. Read less of it

    Instead of consulting the entire conversation for every word, fetch only the handful of earlier moments that actually matter. Less to haul means the slow pipe stops being fatal.

  2. Store less of it

    The cache is bloated — by Movva's estimate it holds ten to a hundred times more than the information in it justifies. Squeeze it and everything downstream gets easier.

  3. Ask for it earlier

    If you can predict what will be needed a few steps ahead, you can start the slow fetch in advance and have it waiting. The delay doesn't disappear — it just happens while other work is going on.

Why he can't just do it

All three are changes to how the model is built — and he doesn't build models. He runs the machines they execute on. He can see the prize clearly, and the lever sits in someone else's hands.
